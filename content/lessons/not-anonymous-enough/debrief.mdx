## Debrief: distinguish removing names from satisfying a disclosure method

### What happened in this case


The export still identifies small groups through combinations of ZIP code, diagnosis and dates. Removing names alone does not resolve this risk.

The original dataset remains coded and reidentifiable because a diagnosis combined with a five-digit ZIP isolates one record and the linkage key is inadequately protected. If you used the exercise, you also saw that transforming one field at a time leaves other distinguishing combinations. Uncontrolled release risks unauthorized disclosure of protected health information.

Increasing the smallest displayed group size reduced uniqueness in this teaching table, but the counter did not approve a release. Safe Harbor or Expert Determination review, or a properly governed limited-data-set pathway, still requires documented institutional review.

The data steward and privacy or research offices define safeguards and agreements. The clinical data owner approves the permitted use and minimum necessary release.

A researcher may need dates to follow a patient’s course and a diagnosis to select relevant cases. Those fields can also distinguish a small group when combined with location information. Review the proposed dataset as a whole, including who can use any linkage key.

Images add other places to inspect. A whole-slide image can include a label image, information stored within the file and a file name derived from an accession. Renaming the file leaves the other locations to be reviewed. Staff need a process for checking the prepared files and holding those that still need attention.

The privacy and research teams can help preserve useful information while reviewing the proposed release. The small-group counter in this exercise answers one question about this table; approval also depends on the permitted use and disclosure method.

### Who does what

Work with the privacy office or research data steward. The person making a technical change should document the test evidence; the responsible clinical or operational lead approves the intended use.
