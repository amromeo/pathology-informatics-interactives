### Count groups before and after changing fields

The controls change a copy of all 18 rows, including any rows your filters are hiding. The smallest-group calculation puts together rows that have the same **birth year, sex, geography, admission period and diagnosis**, counts each group and reports the smallest count. Record labels and study codes are left out of that calculation, so a bigger count cannot tell you whether the key is safe.

For example, after broadening ZIP and diagnosis, NR-001 to NR-004 all have birth year 1988, sex F, geography 021**, admission date 2026-04-18 and diagnosis Malignant neoplasm. That group has four rows. The other groups have four, five and five rows, so the smallest is **4**. Open the [transformed preview](#transformed-privacy-preview) to see the actual rows.

Changing the date to the year alone does not change the count, because all 18 admission dates were the same to begin with. Moving the key somewhere safe does not change it either, because the key is not one of the five fields counted. Both steps can still matter even though the counter does not move.

The three controls each remove a different detail: the exact date within the year, the exact location within a ZIP prefix, and the fact that the cancer is breast cancer. Priya and the investigator have to decide which of those details the study needs. Making all three changes is one cautious proposal for review; it is not a legal requirement for every research file.

### What would allow the file to be shared?

Under **Safe Harbor**, a set list of identifiers must be removed, and the hospital must not actually know that what is left could identify someone. Dates tied to a person can generally keep only the year, and ages over 89 need extra handling. Full ZIP codes cannot stay. A three-digit ZIP prefix can stay only if all the ZIP codes sharing it together cover more than 20,000 people in current Census data; otherwise it is replaced with 000. This exercise shortens ZIP codes but does not check those populations. A study code can also stay under certain conditions on how it is made and used, but its key cannot be shared.

**Expert Determination** needs a qualified expert to assess, and write down, that the risk of identifying anyone is very small, given who will receive the file and what else they could know. The group counter does neither method on its own. Anyone who knows the file was drawn from April 18 admissions already knows the date, even if the column now says only 2026. Priya's review has to take that into account. The [HHS deidentification guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/de-identification/index.html) explains both methods and their conditions.

A **limited data set** can keep dates and some geography, including ZIP codes, once certain direct identifiers are removed. It is still PHI, and it can be used for research, public health or health care operations under a data use agreement. That may keep detail the study needs while limiting who gets it and what they can do with it. A general promise to keep the file confidential is not enough. The [HHS research guidance](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/special-topics/research/index.html) explains limited data sets and the other ways research data can be shared.

For this proposed research release, write down the purpose, the recipient and the agreement needed, and lock down the key. Choosing these controls records a proposed plan in the exercise; it does not approve anything. Priya has to review the whole file and the rules that apply before it is shared. Ask her to check any extra federal or state protections that may apply, including records covered by 42 CFR Part 2.
