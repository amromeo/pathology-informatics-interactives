## Debrief: trace the additional FT4 to its rule

TH-01 displayed the new reference interval, **0.27–4.20 mIU/L**, but revision 8 of the ordering rule still used **0.40** as its lower threshold. Its TSH of **0.30** was below that saved threshold. The order record connects the resulting FT4 to revision 8.

With the approved lower threshold of **0.27**, TH-01 meets neither the lower nor upper condition. Correcting that threshold removes the unintended additions at TH-01, TH-02 and TH-04.

### What the complete replay shows

The original configuration matches **10 of 13** expected actions and creates **five** new FT4 orders. The corrected configuration matches **13 of 13** and creates **two**. TH-03 and TH-07 retain their indicated additions. TH-08 and TH-09 use FT4s already linked to their accessions.

The [boundary examples](#reflex-boundary-examples) explain why including 0.27 or 4.20 would add unintended orders at TH-04 or TH-06. Removing duplicate prevention adds another FT4 at TH-08 and TH-09. Removing the age/order restriction or accepting preliminary results creates different errors. Each setting therefore needs an appropriate record in the test set.

### What the order count leaves unanswered

Fewer new orders can reflect a successful correction or missed indicated tests. Read the full [replay](#reflex-replay-heading) to determine which occurred. The fixed expected action for each record provides the comparison.

For monitoring, define the period and eligible population before counting errors. Include eligible accessions that received no FT4 so that missed additions remain visible. Track review exceptions and unresolved orders as well.

### Prepare the change and affected cases for review

The analyst records which settings changed and retains expected and observed test actions. The director and chemistry staff review the protocol, the follow-up for affected accessions and the checks after use begins.

The main lesson is to follow a change into each record or rule that uses it. Finding the correct interval on the report is one check. The order history and replay establish how the separate ordering rule behaved.

<summary>A closer look: test the meaning of an exact boundary</summary>

A laboratory may approve a limit in a written procedure, then build that limit separately in a report definition and an ordering rule. Each copy can change at a different time. Checking the report’s interval alone leaves the reflex rule untested.

Prepare records just below, exactly at and just above each approved boundary. The exact-boundary records distinguish “below” from “at or below.” Then test the other conditions separately: assay, result status and whether an FT4 order already exists.

Reference-interval builds have a similar translation problem when days, weeks and months are converted into LIS age ranges. In both settings, the laboratory must explain which records belong on each side of a boundary before the analyst writes the condition.
