## Debrief: trace the additional FT4 to its rule

TH-01 displayed the new reference interval, **0.27–4.20 mIU/L**, but revision 8 of the ordering rule still used **0.40** as its lower threshold. Its TSH of **0.30** was below that saved threshold. The order record connects the resulting FT4 to revision 8.

With the approved lower threshold of **0.27**, TH-01 meets neither the lower nor upper condition. Fixing that threshold removes the unwanted FT4s at TH-01, TH-02 and TH-04.

### What the complete replay shows

The original configuration matches **10 of 13** expected actions and creates **five** new FT4 orders. The corrected configuration matches **13 of 13** and creates **two**. TH-03 and TH-07 keep the FT4s they need. TH-08 and TH-09 use FT4s already linked to their accessions.

The [boundary examples](#reflex-boundary-examples) explain why including 0.27 or 4.20 would add unintended orders at TH-04 or TH-06. Removing duplicate prevention adds another FT4 at TH-08 and TH-09. Removing the age and order-type restriction, or accepting preliminary results, causes other errors. So each setting needs its own record in the test set.

### What the order count leaves unanswered

Fewer new orders can mean the fix worked, or that needed tests were missed. Read the full [replay](#reflex-replay-heading) to see which. The fixed expected action for each record provides the comparison.

For monitoring, set the period and the eligible accessions before counting errors. Include eligible accessions that got no FT4, so missed additions still show up. Keep track of exceptions sent for review and orders still open, too.

### Prepare the change and affected cases for review

The analyst records which settings changed and keeps the expected and actual test actions. The director and chemistry staff review the protocol, the follow-up for affected accessions and the checks once the rule is in use.

The main lesson is to follow a change into every record or rule that uses it. Seeing the right interval on the report is one check. The order history and the replay show how the separate ordering rule behaved.

<summary>A closer look: test the meaning of an exact boundary</summary>

A laboratory may approve a limit in a written procedure, then build that limit separately in a report definition and an ordering rule. Each copy can change at a different time. Checking the report’s interval alone leaves the reflex rule untested.

Prepare records just below, exactly at and just above each approved boundary. The exact-boundary records distinguish “below” from “at or below.” Then test the other conditions separately: assay, result status and whether an FT4 order already exists.

Reference intervals have the same kind of problem when days, weeks and months are turned into LIS age ranges. In both cases, the laboratory has to say which records fall on each side of a boundary before the analyst writes the condition.
