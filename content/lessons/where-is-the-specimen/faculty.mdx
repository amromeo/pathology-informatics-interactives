# Faculty guide: Where Is the Specimen?

## At a glance

Allow 25 minutes. Use individually or in a small group. Project the case records and the exercise controls; keep the answer key out of view until learners commit to a decision.

Learning objectives: connect an order to its specimen and worklist; trace the order catalog entry to the worklist; justify the first local check. PIER: 4.1, 4.2, 4.4.

## Run of show

| Minutes | Activity | Ask the group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Read the case and records | What decision has the laboratory been asked to make? |
| 5–18 | Make, test and revise a choice | Which record supports your decision? What could fail? |
| 18–25 | Debrief and retrieval questions | How would you verify this in your own laboratory? |

## Expected answers


Do not let learners relabel or recollect without evidence of an identification failure. The case deliberately separates positive identification from routing logic.

Ask learners to open all four events and distinguish a person documenting receipt from a routing service assigning work. Accessioning is the last documented physical location; the current location remains unknown until staff confirm it. Ask what evidence would establish a subsequent handoff.

Replay the original configuration first: three of five routes pass. SP-EX incorrectly goes to cytology, and an unmatched code goes to the gross room. Change SP-EX alone and rerun to show the remaining exception failure. The complete repair sends SP-EX to the gross room and holds unmatched codes for accessioning review, preserving the other three entries.

Try routing every procedure to the gross room. Cytology and frozen-section checks fail even though the reported excision is corrected. Discuss why adding a new dictionary entry requires a test of that entry, plus neighboring routes, rather than only repeating existing examples.

Select the electronic-receipt shortcut after a passing replay. The final review still fails because it would record a handoff that has not occurred. The approved recovery plan requires accessioning and receiving staff to locate, verify, and document the real container. The LIS team implements the configuration; the laboratory supervisor and pathologist review its clinical meaning and authorize use under local procedures.

## Assess the explanation as well as the selections

Ask the learner to explain why a valid barcode can retrieve the right case while a dictionary lookup assigns it incorrectly. A complete explanation identifies the patient/part check, SP-EX as the input to routing, and revision 17 as the configuration that supplies the destination. “The LIS made an error” is too general to guide a repair.

Ask what the assistant can conclude at 08:40. The answer should distinguish the last documented receipt from the current physical location. Accept possible explanations for the missing handoff, but require the learner to name the evidence needed to choose among them. The case does not establish that the container was physically delivered to cytology.

For the change review, ask the learner to describe expected and observed destinations for all five test cases. A strong answer explains why the unknown-code test adds information beyond the reported excision and why a corrected routing table does not automatically update a previously assigned worklist. In a real LIS, the analyst must verify how configuration changes affect existing cases before selecting a recovery method.

Close with a short handoff to the supervisor: affected case and procedure, observed configuration, last documented location, proposed repair, test results, and outstanding physical recovery. Ask who will confirm receipt and how the original audit trail will be preserved.

## Common misconceptions

- “The patient name by itself.” Ask: what does the record actually establish? Patient identity is necessary but does not distinguish separate ordered tests or specimens. Preserve the accession and specimen relationship through routing.
- “The destination worklist by itself.” Ask: which conclusion would still need evidence? Patient identity is necessary but does not distinguish separate ordered tests or specimens. Preserve the accession and specimen relationship through routing.
- “Reroute every specimen to the same bench.” Ask: what must be checked before making the change? Check requirements, collection instructions and specimen routing before changing a worklist rule.

## Discussion prompts

1. What supports the case decision? A strong answer includes a specific record and the distinction: connect an order to its specimen and worklist.
2. What would you check locally? A strong answer includes: trace the order catalog entry to the worklist, because check requirements, collection instructions and specimen routing before changing a worklist rule.
3. Who needs to participate? A strong answer includes an accessioning supervisor and LIS analyst and separates technical work from clinical approval.

## Group variant

Assign laboratory director, LIS analyst, laboratory operations and clinical service roles. Add clinical informatics and a patient advocate when considering ordering or chart use. Ask each person to state one requirement, one risk and the evidence needed before approval. The director then summarizes what can proceed and what must be resolved.

## Assess transfer

Draw an order-to-specimen-to-worklist trace, comparing one AP and one CP workflow. A strong response names the record, responsible person, first check and an unresolved risk. Use the three learner retrieval questions to distinguish an unsupported shortcut from a reasoned decision.
