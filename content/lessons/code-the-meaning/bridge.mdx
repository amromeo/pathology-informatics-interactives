### Mapping errors happen in real laboratories

A code can exist in LOINC and still describe the wrong test. Published studies show this is a real quality problem, not just a made-up mistake for an exercise.

- **Stram and colleagues (2020):** 90 of 1,916 invited laboratories answered a CAP survey about coagulation and cardiac-marker tests. Of the 275 LOINC codes they submitted, 54 (19.6%) were wrong. Examples included the wrong component, the wrong specimen, or a panel code used for a single result. Only 4.7% of laboratories replied, and only a few tests were asked about, so the results may not apply widely. These are submitted codes, not a count of affected patient results. [Read the study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31603714/).
- **McDonald and colleagues (2023):** a review of 179,537,986 mapped results for 3,029 quantitative tests in two PCORnet DataMarts found errors affecting 4.6% of the mapped results. About half of the mapping errors were in the property part. This percentage counts results in that dataset; it is not the percentage of laboratories making errors. [Read the study abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36343113/).

The two percentages use different denominators and different study designs. Do not average them or read them as a trend over time. One wrong mapping on a commonly ordered test can affect a very large number of results. Neither study tells you the error rate in your own laboratory.

### Check what the code claims

Compare the component, specimen, property, scale, time and, where relevant, method with the local test list. Checking only that the code exists will miss codes that are real but wrong. Use mismatches between unit and property to flag mappings for a closer look; a unit alone cannot confirm the specimen or method. Include common tests and clinically important exceptions in your local review.

Keep mappings you cannot yet support in a work queue where people can see them. Get the assay definition from the person responsible for the assay rather than guessing a nearby code. Record the local code, the LOINC code, the LOINC version, the reason for the choice, the reviewer and the date. Test typical results in the receiving system, including units and interpretations.

If a wrong mapping has already been used, find the affected records and the systems that received them, work out what it means clinically and for any analyses, fix the mapping through the usual change process, and plan how to resend or correct the earlier records. Fixing tomorrow's messages does not fix yesterday's records. Check affected mappings again whenever an assay, the test list or LOINC itself changes.
