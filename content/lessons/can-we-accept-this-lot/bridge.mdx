## Match the question to a statistical test

Start with the design. Were the same specimens measured twice, or are the groups unrelated? Then look at the result: is it a number, or a category such as mutation detected or not detected? Finally, look at how the data are distributed and check the assumptions of the test you have in mind.

**Paired t-test:** keeps each specimen's two results together and analyzes the difference between them. It tests the mean paired difference. In this lot comparison, the differences have no marked outlier and no severe skew, so at this sample size the paired t-test is a reasonable first choice.

**Wilcoxon signed-rank test:** also keeps the pairs together, but works with the signs and ranks of the differences instead of their values. A rank is a value's position when the values are sorted by size. This test can help when paired differences are not normally distributed but are still roughly symmetric. It does not fix strongly skewed differences; symmetry still matters.

A **normal distribution** has a roughly symmetric bell shape. Symmetric means the values spread out in much the same way on either side of the center. Data can be symmetric without being bell-shaped. Look at the paired differences themselves; do not assume they have the same shape as the original measurements.

**Independent-samples t-test:** compares the means of two unrelated groups, such as hemoglobin results from two different groups of patients. There are no pairs to keep together.

**Analysis of variance (ANOVA):** compares a numerical result across three or more groups. For example, it could compare RNA yields from separate tissue samples fixed in three different fixatives. A significant result means the group means are not all the same; further comparisons show which ones differ.

**Fisher exact test:** compares counts in categories, such as mutation detected or not detected in two patient groups. It is useful when the counts are small. Chi-square tests also compare counts, when their assumptions are met.
