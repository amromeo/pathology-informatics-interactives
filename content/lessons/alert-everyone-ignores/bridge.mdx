### Compare the pilot with a ward that did not change

The committee piloted revised guidance on one ward at the beginning of week 3. Weeks 1–2 are the baseline period; weeks 3–4 are the pilot period. Another ward kept the existing guidance. Each of the two general medicine services had about 100 eligible HbA1c order decisions a week. “Eligible” means every decision in the group being studied, whether or not a message was shown.

Reviewers used the same clinical criteria to classify avoidable repeat orders in both wards and periods. The table and trend show those counts per 1,000 decisions. The evaluation includes orders that were kept and orders that were cancelled; it does not pick out only people who clicked the message. These ward numbers stay the same when you change the replay settings.

The pilot ward falls from 80 to 40 avoidable repeats per 1,000. The comparison ward falls from 60 to 50. The difference between their changes is −30 per 1,000: (40 − 80) − (50 − 60). This allows for the change seen on the other ward, but the wards were not chosen at random. Different patients, staffing, other projects running at the same time, different review habits or chance could still explain part of the difference. Four weeks is not long enough to show the effect lasts.

### Look for harm from following the advice

Read safety record SR-14 before recommending anything. A clinician accepted the suggestion and cancelled a test that was needed. A field in the record said the earlier report was usable, but a comment on the report described a limitation that mattered for this decision.

**Automation bias** means relying too readily on a system's suggestion. Here, the review has to look both at the information missing from the display and at how the clinician read the advice. Telling people to “pay more attention” leaves the data and display problem unfixed.

A lower testing rate measures how much testing is done, not how patients are doing. Measure the process, the number of unnecessary repeats as judged by clinical reviewers, the extra work for staff, and any needed tests that were delayed or missed. The one known delay needs investigating; what it meant for the patient is still being reviewed.

With only four to eight avoidable repeats per week in each group, chance alone can produce differences of this size.

### What comparison would strengthen the conclusion?

The calculation is called a difference-in-differences: the change on the trial ward minus the change on the comparison ward. It assumes the comparison ward shows what would have happened without the trial, and two weeks before and after are not enough to test that. A longer interrupted time series looks at changes in both the level and the trend. Assigning wards at random can reduce built-in differences between groups, though staff moving between wards and changes in how work is done still need watching.
