### Count the event that answers the question

The monthly report counts how many times the message was shown. The linked order audit finds 3,000 separate order decisions behind those displays. 12,000 divided by 3,000 is four displays per decision on average, though some decisions had more and some fewer.

400 clicks out of 12,000 displays is a 3.3% click rate. That measures how often people clicked the message, nothing more. To find out whether ordering changed, link each message to one order decision and look at the separate record of whether the order was cancelled or kept. To find out whether that was the right call, look at the clinical context.

Click rates by role need care too. A nurse collecting a specimen and a resident choosing tests are doing different jobs. A difference in their click rates does not show that one group makes better decisions.

### Cut unnecessary interruptions without hiding new decisions

Messages that come too often, do not apply or keep repeating make people pay less attention to alerts, a problem called **alert fatigue**. In this case, showing the message only to the ordering clinician, and only when there is relevant history, removes messages that have nothing to do with the person's decision. Not showing the same message again for the same decision removes more.

Holding back repeat messages needs a clear limit. Reopening the same order panel does not raise a new clinical question; a later decision, after the patient's situation has changed, may. The last replay event is a new decision for the first patient's encounter, so it has to be checked again.

A suggestion beside the order can show the earlier information without anything to dismiss. A soft stop demands attention but lets the clinician carry on. A hard stop blocks the order; this case's criteria do not justify that.

Change one control at a time and look at the event cards. The replay simply applies your settings to these ten events. Fewer messages in this small set do not predict how many tests a hospital would avoid.
