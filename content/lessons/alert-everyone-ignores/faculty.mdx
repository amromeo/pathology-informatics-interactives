# Faculty guide: Evaluating a Repeat-Test Alert

## At a glance

Allow 40 minutes. Use individually or in a small group. Project the case records and the exercise controls; keep the answer key out of view until learners commit to a decision.

Learning objectives: compare pilot changes with concurrent changes elsewhere; define eligible decisions and balancing measures; justify the first local check. PIER: 6.3, 6.4.

## Learning goals and sequence

PIER 6.3–6.4 are addressed through analyzing CDS limitations and evaluating an intervention. Allow about 40 minutes. Begin with the monthly report, ask what the numerator and denominator represent, then reveal the linked audit. Use the replay to compare targeting, recipient, repetition, and format. Reserve the final 15 minutes for the comparison ward and SR-14.

API/Harrison Session 7, slides 28–45, supports discussion of alert fatigue, automation bias, and evaluation. Avoid turning the lesson into a search for a universally correct acceptance rate.

## Expected reasoning

The monthly rate is 400/12,000 = 3.3% of presentations acknowledged. It is not the fraction of patients with improved care. There are 3,000 distinct linked decisions, so the mean is four presentations per decision. Role-specific counts cannot rank clinical performance without examining the different work performed.

With relevant history, ordering clinicians, and once-per-decision presentation, events E01, E07, E08, and E10 receive guidance. E02 and E09 are repeat presentations; E03 is collection staff; E04–E06 lack qualifying history. An inline format yields four presentations and zero mandatory interruptions. Do not extrapolate these selected events to August.

E08 and E10 preserve documented clinical reasons to proceed. A hard stop produces documented-reason blocks. Ask why suppressing for an entire admission would incorrectly hide E10.

## Quantitative discussion

Before: pilot 16/200 and comparison 12/200. After: pilot 8/200 and comparison 10/200. Rates per 1,000 are 80→40 and 60→50, respectively. The difference between changes is −30 per 1,000.

Do not describe this as a proven causal effect. Discuss case mix, concurrent work, selection of wards, consistency of clinical adjudication, and the short time series. Ask what longer follow-up and uncertainty estimates would add.

SR-14 requires investigation and correction before expansion. The report field and narrative comment conflict in relevance to the current use. Strong answers address data provenance, display, usability testing, clinical review, and patient follow-up, rather than blaming the clinician alone.

## Assessment and governance

Ask each learner to propose four linked measures and a review interval. The selected checklist is a minimum set, not a complete protocol. Require a named clinical reviewer, technical maintainer, safety escalation route, and criteria to pause or retire the intervention. “More acceptance” and “fewer tests” are insufficient by themselves.

## Common misconceptions

- “Attribute the whole pilot decrease to the alert.” Ask: what does the record actually establish? The concurrent comparison helps distinguish a pilot-associated change from a broader trend. Small counts and nonrandom assignment limit causal claims.
- “Ignore the comparison service after go-live.” Ask: which conclusion would still need evidence? The concurrent comparison helps distinguish a pilot-associated change from a broader trend. Small counts and nonrandom assignment limit causal claims.
- “Count dismissed alerts as prevented repeats.” Ask: what must be checked before making the change? A rate requires a denominator; evaluation also needs justified repeats, missed needed testing and workflow burden.

## Discussion prompts

1. What supports the case decision? A strong answer includes a specific record and the distinction: compare pilot changes with concurrent changes elsewhere.
2. What would you check locally? A strong answer includes: define eligible decisions and balancing measures, because a rate requires a denominator; evaluation also needs justified repeats, missed needed testing and workflow burden.
3. Who needs to participate? A strong answer includes a quality lead and clinical informatics analyst and separates technical work from clinical approval.

## Group variant

Assign laboratory director, LIS analyst, laboratory operations and clinical service roles. Add clinical informatics and a patient advocate when considering ordering or chart use. Ask each person to state one requirement, one risk and the evidence needed before approval. The director then summarizes what can proceed and what must be resolved.

## Assess transfer

Draft a pilot evaluation table with pre/post groups, denominators and safety measures. A strong response names the record, responsible person, first check and an unresolved risk. Use the three learner retrieval questions to distinguish an unsupported shortcut from a reasoned decision.
