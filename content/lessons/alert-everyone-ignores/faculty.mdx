# Faculty guide: Evaluating a Repeat-Test Alert

## Learning goals and sequence

PIER 6.3–6.4 are addressed through analyzing CDS limitations and evaluating an intervention. Allow about 40 minutes. Begin with the monthly report, ask what the numerator and denominator represent, then reveal the linked audit. Use the replay to compare targeting, recipient, repetition, and format. Reserve the final 15 minutes for the comparison ward and SR-14.

API/Harrison Session 7, slides 28–45, supports discussion of alert fatigue, automation bias, and evaluation. Avoid turning the lesson into a search for a universally correct acceptance rate.

## Expected reasoning

The monthly rate is 400/12,000 = 3.3% of presentations acknowledged. It is not the fraction of patients with improved care. There are 3,000 distinct linked decisions, so the mean is four presentations per decision. Role-specific counts cannot rank clinical performance without examining the different work performed.

With relevant history, ordering clinicians, and once-per-decision presentation, events E01, E07, E08, and E10 receive guidance. E02 and E09 are repeat presentations; E03 is collection staff; E04–E06 lack qualifying history. An inline format yields four presentations and zero mandatory interruptions. Do not extrapolate these selected events to August.

E08 and E10 preserve documented clinical reasons to proceed. A hard stop produces unsafe blocks. Ask why suppressing for an entire admission would incorrectly hide E10.

## Quantitative discussion

Before: pilot 160/2,000 and comparison 120/2,000. After: pilot 80/2,000 and comparison 100/2,000. Rates per 1,000 are 80→40 and 60→50, respectively. The difference between changes is −30 per 1,000.

Do not describe this as a proven causal effect. Discuss case mix, concurrent work, selection of wards, consistency of clinical adjudication, and the short time series. Ask what longer follow-up and uncertainty estimates would add.

SR-14 requires investigation and correction before expansion. The report field and narrative comment conflict in relevance to the current use. Strong answers address data provenance, display, usability testing, clinical review, and patient follow-up, rather than blaming the clinician alone.

## Assessment and governance

Ask each learner to propose four linked measures and a review interval. The selected checklist is a minimum set, not a complete protocol. Require a named clinical reviewer, technical maintainer, safety escalation route, and criteria to pause or retire the intervention. “More acceptance” and “fewer tests” are insufficient by themselves.

## Preparing discussion with API slides

Use [Association for Pathology Informatics teaching slides: Clinical Decision Support (PowerPoint)](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/assets/lms/jimharrison/PathInformatics%207%20CDS%20slides.pptx) by James H. Harrison, Jr., and Ronald Jackups, slides 33–34, 42–44, to prepare the discussion after residents have explained their case decision. Ask: Compare alert acceptance with process and patient outcomes. Identify what the audit must establish before a click can count as an appropriate change in ordering. Have residents support their answer with a specific record or result from this lesson before comparing it with the slides.

These slides provide teaching background. Use the lesson's primary sources and current local policies for clinical criteria, legal requirements, and approval decisions; do not turn a slide example or numerical recommendation into a local requirement. If the PowerPoint link changes, locate the session on the [API teaching-slide page](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/teaching-slide-sets). The lesson and discussion remain usable without the external file.
