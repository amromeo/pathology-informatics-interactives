## Debrief: a quieter alert still needs clinical evaluation

The original monthly report mixed repeated presentations and different staff roles. Its “Accept” button recorded acknowledgement, so the 3.3% rate did not measure appropriate ordering.

In the replay, selecting relevant prior information, the ordering clinician, and one presentation per decision reduces ten presentations to four. Making those suggestions inline removes mandatory interruptions in this event set. The new decision at 10:00 still receives review; suppression does not last for the entire admission.

These counts describe software behavior in a small audit sample. They do not demonstrate fewer unnecessary tests, safer care, or a predicted monthly reduction. A soft stop could be considered if its added burden were justified. A hard stop conflicts with the case's clinical discretion and exception requirements.

### Interpret the comparison and the safety event together

The pilot ward's decrease was 40 avoidable repeats per 1,000 decisions, while the comparison ward decreased by 10. Their changes differed by 30 per 1,000. That finding is more informative than the pilot-only change, but remains an observational comparison with a short follow-up.

SR-14 shows why success cannot be defined by clicking or cancelling. Investigate the delayed indicated test with the treating team, inspect how the report comment was omitted, assess the patient's course, and correct the rule inputs and display. Replay cases with incomplete or conflicting source information before reassessing a limited pilot.

The review plan should include unique decisions and actual order changes; clinically reviewed avoidable repeats; message burden and time by role; and delayed or missed needed testing. Assign responsibility for reviewing safety events and define when the intervention should be paused, revised, or retired. A completed checklist supports this review process; it is not a deployment approval.
