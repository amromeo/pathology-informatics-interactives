# Evaluating a Repeat-Test Alert

You are reviewing hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ordering guidance with the laboratory director and the clinical decision support (CDS) committee. The August report shows the message was displayed 12,000 times, and “Accept” was clicked 400 times. Staff say they are interrupted constantly. The committee asks whether to redesign the guidance, and how to tell whether a redesign helps.

The message appears for every HbA1c order, and can come back each time the order panel is reopened. Collection staff get it too. Clicking “Accept” just closes the message; cancelling a test is a separate step. So the report cannot tell you how often ordering changed, or whether a change was the right one.

The criteria stay the same throughout this case: show a usable final report collected within 90 days, or a pending order, and always let the clinician go ahead for a clinical reason. These are this hospital's review criteria, not a general rule for how often to test.

## Terms used in this case

- **Alert fatigue:** paying less attention to alerts because they come too often, do not apply or keep repeating.
- **Order decision:** one clinician deciding about one order. A single decision can see the same message several times.
- **Trial ward and comparison ward:** the ward that got the new guidance, and a similar ward that kept the old guidance over the same weeks.
- **Avoidable repeat:** a repeat order that clinical reviewers, using agreed criteria, judged unnecessary.
- **Automation bias:** trusting a system's suggestion too readily.

## Your task

Compare the monthly message report with the linked order audit. Then change who gets the guidance and when, and replay ten events. Finally, look at a four-week comparison between two wards and a review of a delayed test, and recommend what should happen next.

## What you will be able to do

- Turn a count of messages into a count of order decisions before judging an alert.
- Change who gets an alert, and how often, without hiding new decisions or blocking justified orders.
- Compare the change on a trial ward with the change on a comparison ward, and say what the difference can and cannot show.
- Choose measures of benefit, extra work and harm, including needed tests that were delayed.
