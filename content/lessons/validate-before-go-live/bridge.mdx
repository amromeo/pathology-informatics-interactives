## Read the packet without deleting the failures

The fixed-tissue set has 60 cases: 20 gastrointestinal biopsies, 15 skin cases, 15 breast cases and 10 resections from other organs. It includes small foci, low- and high-grade lesions, benign look-alikes and difficult preparations. Rao read 30 and Chen read 30; each compared their own screen and glass readings 14 days apart. No cases were dropped after reading. The table counts one pair of readings per case, not slides or fields.

The early frozen-section set has 17 separate cases read eight days apart. It has three disagreements and is not a finished validation. Do not add it to the fixed-tissue series to justify approval. Home workstations H1 and H2, and the remote network connection, have no study records at all.

The two fixed-tissue disagreements stay in the 60-case denominator after review. Rescanning a slide can test a fix, but the new image must not quietly replace the original study result. Keep the scan version, the original readings, the investigation, the repeat checks and the approval decision.

For fixed tissue at the hospital, the packet records scanner S1, viewer 3.2, workstation D1, both trained readers, and checks of identity, tissue coverage, retrieval and access to glass. The plan has to keep those checks. This lesson asks what more evidence is needed; ticking boxes is not evidence in itself.

## Keep device approval separate from laboratory approval

In the United States, check the current labeling and regulatory status of the exact device, the laboratory's accreditation requirements and local responsibilities. The Food and Drug Administration's original DEN160056 decision is an example of a defined system and scope: fixed paraffin-embedded surgical tissue, with frozen sections, cytology and hematopathology on non-paraffin material outside that original indication. This older example says nothing about every device on the market today.

A local validation does not widen what the manufacturer is allowed to claim. And a device's approval does not show how it performs in every local setting. Check the actual scanner, software, screen settings, location and use, and ask the hospital's regulatory advisers when the proposed use goes beyond the approval. Other countries have their own rules.

After approval, keep records of versions, settings, users, training, quality checks and how to fall back to glass. A change that could affect how images are captured or displayed needs its effect reviewed and the right level of checking or revalidation. A new clinical use needs much more than checking that people can still log in.

### Reporting from home and the tested workstation

CMS memo QSO-23-15-CLIA, revised October 14, 2026, continues to allow, under certain conditions, remote review of digital laboratory material under the main laboratory's CLIA certificate, including from a private home. The main laboratory's director remains responsible, reports have to name the remote site, and the memo's other conditions apply. Since the earlier allowance ended on March 23, 2026, remote review of digital cytology needs its own appropriate CLIA certificate.

That federal policy says nothing about whether this home workstation, network, screen or fallback to glass has been validated. The lesson's hold stays in place until the proposed setup has the local evidence it needs. Check the current memo and any state requirements again before going ahead.
