## Separate acquisition from enlargement

A scanner records light from the slide on a grid. **Pixel spacing** is the real distance between neighboring pixels. Smaller spacing means more samples across the same piece of tissue. How fine a detail the scanner can actually show also depends on the lenses, focus, slide preparation and sensor; spacing alone does not tell you the smallest structure you will be able to see.

A scanner's “20×” or “40×” label describes how the slide was scanned. Zoom in the viewer is how much the picture is enlarged on screen. They are not the same thing. Enlarging an image does not add detail. Extra focal planes can help with thick material, but they take longer to scan and add data, and they do not make every kind of preparation suitable for diagnosis on screen.

**Downsampling** replaces a larger grid with a smaller one. **Lossy compression** throws some information away to make the file smaller. **Lossless compression** makes the file smaller in a way that gives back exactly the pixels it was given. Saving an image that is already blurred, or already lossy, in a lossless format does not bring back what was lost.

### Relating sampling to familiar magnification labels

About 0.5 micrometers per pixel is commonly called 20× equivalent; 0.25 micrometers per pixel is commonly called 40× equivalent. These are handy comparisons, not a promise that the optical detail is the same. A pyramid of lower-resolution copies, each half the width and height of the one before, adds roughly a third to the uncompressed size of the full-resolution image. The calculator's base size leaves out the pyramid, metadata and file-system overhead.
