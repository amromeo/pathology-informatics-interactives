## Estimate storage before choosing a policy

For a separate planning example, take a rectangle of tissue **15 mm wide and 10 mm high**, in RGB color with three 8-bit channels, and assume no space is saved on blank areas. At 0.5 µm/pixel the grid is 30,000 × 20,000 pixels. Each pixel uses three bytes before compression. At 0.25 µm/pixel each dimension doubles, so pixel count and base-layer bytes quadruple.

The calculator assumes 500 slides per working day and 250 working days per year: 125,000 slides annually. One or three focal planes contain the same number of pixels per plane. Keeping two copies doubles the storage; having a second copy is not the same as having a backup plan. A 10:1 ratio means the stored file is estimated at one tenth of the raw size. Real compression depends on the tissue, the compression method and its settings. These ratios are assumptions for planning, not clinical recommendations and not measured from the image panels.

Decimal GB means one billion bytes; TB means one trillion. Transfer time assumes the whole full-resolution file travels over an otherwise empty 100 megabit/second connection, at eight bits per byte. It ignores network overhead, other traffic, decoding and delay. Viewers usually fetch just the tiles on screen rather than downloading the whole slide first. Pyramids, metadata, labels, indexes, rescans and keeping images longer than a year all add storage not counted here.

## Match the file and viewing system to the task

A still photograph shows one chosen field and may leave out the tissue around it. Live telepathology sends a moving view, so moving around the slide, focus, network delay and who is driving the microscope all matter. Whole-slide imaging captures an area you can move around in, often at several resolutions, but whether all the tissue was found and in focus still has to be checked.

JPEG is usually lossy. TIFF can hold tiles and several resolutions, but the file extension does not tell you the compression or whether a viewer can open it. DICOM, Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine, defines images and the information that goes with them for sharing between systems. Its current whole-slide format stores tiles as frames and can hold one level of a resolution pyramid. It is not a compression ratio, and it does not guarantee images are shared correctly.

After an image is exported and retrieved, check that it is still linked to the right accession, block and slide, and check the pixel spacing, scan settings, compression history and that all the tissue is there. DICOM records whether lossy compression was used; converting a file must not wipe that record. These are the same kinds of checks as in the interface lessons.

Screen size and pixel density affect how much tissue you see at a given scale. Brightness, color settings, calibration, room lighting and viewing angle affect what you notice. More screen pixels alone do not make a screen fit for reporting. Check the screen and the room with the approved viewer and the way pathologists actually work.
