## Debrief: place decision support at the decision it can improve

### What happened in this case

The ordering audit showed a problem with how the guidance reached people: an inbox message arrived after the order was signed, and it also went to collection staff. Changing the clinical rule alone would not fix either problem.

In the builder, the right information was the dated report or pending order, the right person was the ordering clinician, the right channel was the order screen and the right time was before signing. A suggestion beside the order left the decision with the clinician without anything to dismiss. A soft stop also let the clinician continue, but added an interruption that would need a good reason.

A hard stop failed the specification, because the local rule did not allow blocking every repeat. The encounter with a documented clinical reason shows why. Cancelling an order is not automatically a success, and continuing is not automatically a failure. The audit should keep the decision, the earlier order or report that was shown, and the reason.

### What still needs clinical and technical review

The laboratory director and the clinical CDS committee approve the clinical criteria and how exceptions are handled. The EHR and laboratory teams check that the right patient and test are matched, and check report status, collection times, pending-order status, comments and where the "usable" information comes from. Before the guidance goes live, they also test corrected reports, out-of-date data, missing fields and changes in the patient's condition.

A screen that says "usable" is only as reliable as the data behind it. The next lesson looks at a suggestion that was accepted and delayed a test that was needed, because important context was not stored in a field the rule could read.

A monitored trial should link each order decision to the message shown and what the clinician did, measure the extra work, review whether the decisions were appropriate and look for needed tests that were delayed. Eight encounters passing is evidence about those eight checks; it is not approval to go live.

<summary>A closer look: choose the moment when the order can change</summary>

A clinician can rethink a repeat test while still looking at the order. The same advice sent after signing means finding and changing an order already placed. Sending it to collection staff gives it to people who cannot make that decision.

Useful guidance shows the earlier report or pending order, reaches the clinician making the decision and appears while the choice is still open. A suggestion next to the order can give that information without making every clinician dismiss an alert. A soft stop adds an interruption, and whether it is worth it needs checking.

A conference example about repeated daily magnesium orders shows one way to use a default setting in an alert. This case needs its own choice of design, with an easy way to order a repeat when one is needed.

**Before a pilot:** Which encounter would you use to check that a clinician can still order a repeat when the patient’s condition has changed?

### Who does what

The EHR team builds and tests the guidance and records the results. The laboratory director and a representative of the clinicians who order the test agree on the clinical criteria and approve the design.
