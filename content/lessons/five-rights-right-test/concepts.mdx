### How clinical decision support reaches an order

Clinical decision support (CDS) is information or an action that helps someone make a decision about care. In this case, the EHR looks up the patient's earlier laboratory report and any pending orders, applies an approved rule and shows guidance. The clinician reads it, decides whether to go ahead, and the order audit records what happened.

Each step can go wrong on its own. A correct rule can read the wrong patient's report. The right patient can still have a result that is unusable or out of date. A good suggestion can arrive too late to help. Testing the screen alone will not show that the data and rule behind it are right.

### Apply the five rights to this decision

The **five rights of CDS** are a way to check the information, the person, the format, the channel and the timing of support. Here they become five practical design questions:

- **Information:** Does the clinician see the dated report or pending order, its status, and enough context to judge whether it matters? A long policy does not answer the question about this patient.
- **Person:** Can the person who sees it act on this ordering decision? The nurse collecting the sample has a different job from the clinician ordering the test.
- **Format:** Should the information be a suggestion next to the order, an interruption that still lets the clinician continue, or a hard stop? The approved policy leaves the decision to the clinician.
- **Channel:** Does the guidance appear in the order screen where the test is chosen, or in a separate inbox?
- **Timing:** Does it arrive before the clinician signs, when changing the order is still easy?

Format is how the support is presented; channel is where it shows up. A suggestion beside the order and a soft stop can both appear inside the EHR, but they ask for very different amounts of attention.

### Match the support to the thinking needed

Recognizing a familiar pattern can be quick and almost automatic, often called Type I thinking. Deliberately comparing an earlier result, the reason for testing and the current clinical question takes more effort, often called Type II thinking. These describe kinds of mental work, not two kinds of clinician.

A short, clear display can make the relevant facts easier to check. Being interrupted over and over can instead train people to click past messages without reading them. Do not assume a clinician ignored the evidence just because they closed a badly timed message.

CDS also includes order sets, links to relevant references, result summaries, documentation prompts and laboratory protocols. For example, an order set can make the right first tests easier to find, and an approved reflex protocol can add a follow-up test when set criteria are met. An interrupting alert is just one option among several.

### Fit the support to how people work

The order composer is the screen where a clinician chooses and edits an order. A soft stop interrupts that work but lets the clinician carry on; a hard stop blocks them under set conditions. An inline suggestion puts information next to the order without anything to dismiss.

Good decision support has to be fast enough for the task, fit the way people work and make the next step obvious. Keep rules simple, use data that already exist, ask for more information only when it would change the decision, and offer a useful alternative rather than an unexplained stop. Watch what the rules do and keep them up to date. These principles come from Bates and colleagues; how to apply them depends on the laboratory problem.
