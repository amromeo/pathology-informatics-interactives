# Debrief

## A message reaching the EHR is only one part of go-live

The successful transmission test answers a narrow question: can a message move from the analyzer, through the LIS and interface, into the EHR? It does not show that clinicians can find the right order, that the specimen requirements are correct, that the result is displayed properly, that corrections behave safely, or that the laboratory is ready to run the assay around the clock.

For this assay, go-live requires all of those pieces. Passing the interface test does not cancel the open report, CDS, SOP, training, or staffing work.

## The groups do different work

- **Enterprise IT** runs the shared infrastructure: servers, networks, identity and access services, security controls, and enterprise communications.
- **The laboratory IT team, often called the LIS team,** is the technical team closest to the laboratory applications. Its analysts enter approved test definitions, configure LIS rules and interfaces, move changes through build and test environments, support users, and investigate failures. The LIS team performs the build; it does not independently decide the clinical content of the test or report.
- **Pathology informatics** works between laboratory practice and the technical build. It asks whether the order, specimen workflow, result structure, display, rules, interfaces, and downstream uses fit together. It also helps the laboratory use the same naming, result, correction, and testing practices across sections. That does not mean the pathology informaticist personally enters every field in the LIS.
- **Laboratory administration** makes the service operational: procedures, staffing, training, supplies, coverage, and day-to-day management.
- **The pathologist or laboratory director** has medical responsibility for the service and approves the clinical content and performance of the order and report.

One person may hold more than one of these roles. The distinction is about the work and the approval, not the job title on a badge.

## The laboratory report is still a laboratory product in the EHR

The report includes more than the numeric result. It includes the test identity, result fields, units, reference information, comments, flags, and correction history. The laboratory must review the report in the downstream system where clinicians use it, not just in the LIS.

This matters because the EHR is often managed outside the laboratory. The stewardship literature makes the practical point that clinicians still look to the laboratory for reliable laboratory information even when the laboratory does not control the EHR. Moving the report into another system changes who must collaborate; it does not remove the laboratory's responsibility to check the report and correct problems.

## Some problems cross the LIS–EHR boundary

The laboratory record is organized around the order, specimen, method, testing, verification, and correction. The EHR combines the result with encounters, diagnoses, medications, trend displays, messages, and CDS. A problem may begin in one record and appear in the other, so the investigation may need both views.

The support tickets show how to start:

- If the laboratory report itself is wrong, pathology and the laboratory lead. The LIS team usually makes the approved technical correction.
- If the report is correct but a downstream EHR trend graph groups it with the wrong assay, the clinical informatics or EHR team leads the display investigation. Pathology confirms what the result represents and how it should be grouped.
- If a corrected result causes an EHR alert to fire again, the work is shared. Pathology informatics supplies the laboratory workflow and meaning of the correction. Clinical informatics and the clinical service supply the broader EHR and patient-care workflow. The group must decide what the alert should do and then test that behavior.

Pathology informatics can design and govern laboratory-related CDS. Clinical informatics is not the sole owner of CDS, and shared CDS work does not transfer ownership of the laboratory report away from the laboratory.
