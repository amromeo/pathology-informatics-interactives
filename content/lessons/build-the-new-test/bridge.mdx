### Test the version you intend to release

The build system is where settings are made; the test system is where they are checked under control; the live system is used for patient care. Record the version, and anything it depends on, that moves from one to the next. Re-entering settings by hand in the live system can introduce differences from the version that passed testing.

The eight cases cover positive, negative, more than one virus, failed, pending, unsuitable specimen, corrected and repeat-sent results. Each expected result comes from the case specification. Validation means comparing what the system produced with what it should have produced, not just showing that a message arrived.

Run the checks after every change. Changing a control clears the last test result and your readiness choices. That stands for checking the evidence again after a build changes; it does not mean every real change needs every unrelated test repeated.

### Write a recommendation, not a ticked box

The readiness items below are work your recommendation should cover. Ticking them does not approve anything. The laboratory director reviews what the results mean with the clinical service; analysts set up and test the systems; laboratory staff check the bench procedure; information technology and clinical informatics staff look after their own systems and EHR screens.

For a real assay, find out the manufacturer's instructions, its regulatory status and what the laboratory has to do to verify its performance before choosing a testing plan. Testing the information systems is separate from checking the assay itself. The manufacturer's approval, or a vendor's interface test, does not show that the local report is right.

The go-live plan should name the version going live, who can approve it, the trained staff, who provides support, the downtime process, how to go back to the old version, and who reviews the first live reports. If a new report turns out wrong after go-live, stop or limit the affected work, keep the evidence, fix the problem and check the reports already sent.

For an unmodified FDA-cleared or approved assay, CLIA requires verification of the manufacturer's accuracy, precision and reportable range, and confirmation that reference intervals suit the laboratory's patients (42 CFR 493.1253(b)(1)). Modified or laboratory-developed tests require establishing performance specifications, including analytical sensitivity and specificity (493.1253(b)(2)). In this lesson, validating the build means testing the LIS, interface and EHR settings against the reports they should produce.
