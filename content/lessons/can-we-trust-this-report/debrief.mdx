## Debrief: What should the director take to the quality meeting?

The [original summary table](#tat-original-summary) compared 210 pre-go-live cases with 245 cases accessioned after go-live. Its mean rose from 3.3 to 3.8 working days, while its median fell from 2.7 to 2.5. Either number alone would give an incomplete account.

The weekly review located the largest delay in cutover week. Its 35 cases had a mean of 8.4 and a median of 9.5 working days. In the six following weeks, 210 cases had a mean of 3.0 and a median of 2.5. Those later values were slightly lower than the overall pre-go-live values. The baseline itself varied, so this comparison does not establish that the new LIS caused an improvement.

Show all three periods: pre-go-live, cutover week and the six weeks after cutover. Keep all 455 cases. The unusual week matters to the laboratory and to the patients who experienced the delays. Separating that week makes the event visible; deleting the long cases would conceal it.

## What happened to the missing accession times?

Twenty-seven structured LIS fields were blank. The original accession times still existed on requisitions and in the paper cutover log. The coordinator used those sources to calculate TAT. The accessioning supervisor confirmed that paper accessioning occurred at receipt, so the received stamp and paper log described the intended start event.

In the [case-list table](#tat-case-table), these cases retain both the blank LIS field and the recovered time. That distinction lets another reviewer reproduce the calculation and see what remains incomplete in the LIS. Using the later data-entry time would shorten the measured delay.

The first eight cutover cases had structured times; the other 27 needed the source-document review. All 35 belonged in the report. For future reports, accessioning staff and the LIS analyst should check that original accession times are captured in the required field.

## What should be checked before the next report?

The coordinator prepares the spreadsheet. The analyst explains which records and fields the export selects. The resident compares the spreadsheet with the accession records and works through a calculation. The surgical pathology director reviews the definitions, unusual periods and conclusions before the report is used.

The central questions are practical: which cases are included, where did each time come from, and what does the chosen comparison show? The seven data-quality checks help organize that review. This is the data-literacy work described by PIER 2.1 and 1.2.
