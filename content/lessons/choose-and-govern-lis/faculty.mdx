# Faculty guide: Selecting and Maintaining a Laboratory Information System

## At a glance

Allow 40 minutes. The lesson works for one resident or a small group. Project the purchasing summary and the demonstration records, and keep the answers below out of view until residents have committed to their classifications.

By the end, residents should be able to tell a failed demonstration from an unfinished one, explain why the panel's ratings cannot override a requirement, compare costs once every total includes the same items, and say what the laboratory has to do at go-live, at each upgrade, after an outage and before retiring the old system. The lesson covers PIER objectives 8.3 and 8.4.

## Run of show

| Minutes | Activity | Ask the group |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5 | Read the case and the purchasing summary | On the ratings alone, which vendor would you pick? |
| 5–15 | Classify the nine demonstration records | Which of these did someone actually see working? |
| 15–25 | Move the weights, then add the missing costs | Did anything change about who met the requirements? |
| 25–33 | Choose the four actions from go-live to retirement | What could go wrong if the laboratory skipped this? |
| 33–40 | Make a recommendation and read the debrief | Would you take Birch forward now, or wait? What would you need to see first? |

## Expected answers

**Demonstration records.** Alder: correction failed, recovery met, export not yet shown. Birch: all three met. Cedar: correction not yet shown, recovery met, export failed. A promised patch is not a retest. A demonstration that stopped partway is unfinished, not failed.

**Scores.** With weights of 50, 30 and 20: Alder 4.8, Birch 3.2, Cedar 3.8. With all the weight on training: 4, 4 and 3. With every weight at zero there is no score. No setting changes who met the requirements.

**Costs, in thousands of US dollars.** Installation plus five years of service: Alder 180 + 5 × 80 = 580; Birch 240 + 5 × 65 = 565; Cedar 160 + 5 × 70 = 510. With interfaces, data migration and exit added: 695, 695 and 720. Alder's interface figure is the committee's estimate, not a quote.

**From go-live to retirement.** Test with local interfaces and staff before go-live. Check what the upgrade changes and test it before installing. Get support, change notice, the exit export and post-outage rechecking written into the hosting contract before signing. Confirm old reports can be found before switching off the old server.

**Recommendation.** Birch can go forward to contract talks and testing on the laboratory's own systems. Waiting is also acceptable if the resident names what is still missing, who will get it and by when. Neither answer is permission to buy.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Alder scored highest, so Alder wins."** Ask: what happened when Alder corrected a report? Send them back to script C-2.
- **"Cedar didn't finish, so Cedar failed."** Ask: what did the record actually show? An unfinished demonstration needs another session, not a fail.
- **"The vendor will patch it, so mark it as met."** Ask: has anyone seen the patched version work?
- **"Cedar is the cheapest."** Ask: which costs are missing from the opening totals?
- **"The vendor tested the upgrade, so it's safe to install tonight."** Ask: whose reports, interfaces and screens did the vendor test?

## Discussion prompts

1. Alder's correction failed at the demonstration. What would you need to see before considering Alder again? *A strong answer:* the fixed version, retested on the same script, ideally with one of the laboratory's own reports.
2. Someone on the committee suggests treating corrections as "important but not required." What should happen? *A strong answer:* a change to the requirements needs a clinical reason and the director's approval, and then every vendor is rechecked against the new list, not just Alder.
3. Who at your hospital would have to agree before the laboratory signs a contract? *A strong answer:* the laboratory director for the clinical requirements, IT and purchasing for the contract, and the LIS team for installation and testing.

## Group variant

Give residents roles: laboratory director, LIS analyst, bench supervisor, a clinician who reads the reports, and someone from purchasing. Each names the one requirement they would refuse to give up and the one cost or risk that worries them most. The director then says what can go forward and what has to wait.

## Assess transfer

Ask each resident to write one requirement from their own laboratory as something a vendor could be watched doing, and the demonstration that would show whether it works. A strong answer names a real task, what success looks like on screen, and who would approve it.

## Using the API teaching slides

After residents have explained their decision, [System Implementation and Management](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/assets/lms/jimharrison/PathInformatics%209%20Impl-Mgmt%20slides.pptx) by James H. Harrison, Jr. (slides 16–20 and 22–25) works well for discussion. Ask residents to take one attractive vendor claim and turn it into a laboratory task they could watch at a demonstration, supporting their answer with a record from this lesson. The slides are background; local policy and the primary sources decide clinical and legal questions. If the link moves, the session is listed on the [API teaching-slide page](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/teaching-slide-sets).

- 8.3: choosing a new LIS on what the demonstrations showed rather than on ratings.
- 8.4: keeping the system and its records dependable from installation to retirement.
