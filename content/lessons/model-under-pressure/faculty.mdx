# Faculty guide: Model Under Pressure

## At a glance

Allow 40 minutes for the core exercise and more time if learners are new to machine learning or quantitative comparisons. Assume basic pathology and routine laboratory knowledge. Explain unfamiliar informatics step by step, with the case and worked calculations. A learner should be able to describe what the model does, what it gets wrong and how the team would find those mistakes.

The lesson covers PIER 2.4 through four proposals, local slide results, monthly monitoring, a prevalence calculator and questions about patient use. The extra explanation is part of the teaching; it should not be treated as optional background that learners must already know.

## Run of show

If the first ten minutes are unfamiliar, extend that discussion rather than rushing through the definitions. The expandable notes offer more detail about training and the calculator's formula. The essential explanation is visible without opening them.

## Explain the unfamiliar computing steps

A model is a set of calculations. Parameters are adjustable numbers in those calculations. In supervised training, the model's predictions are compared with supplied answers, and parameters are adjusted to reduce errors. Testing uses separate cases. “Supervised” describes the use of labels during training; it does not promise continuous human review during use.

Before the local test, pathologists agree on the slide diagnoses before the model is run. Those diagnoses serve as the reference standard. Make this comparison explicit: the pathologists' diagnosis establishes whether metastasis is present; the model's call determines whether it found or missed that metastasis.

## Expected answers

**Proposals:** P1 is a programmed rule; check its limits and exceptions, including missing control results. P2 is supervised image detection; compare its marks on new local slides with the pathologists' diagnoses. P3 is unsupervised clustering; compare diagnoses, gene activity and sample-preparation methods within each cluster. P4 generates text; compare each statement and any omissions with the original reports.

Supervised describes training, while generative describes output. A model trained on reports paired with clinician-written summaries could use supervised training and generate text. The slide proposal explicitly describes labeled-image training. The summary proposal describes text output without specifying the training method. Learners choose the description supported by each proposal, then a first check that could reveal mistakes.

**[Table 1](../../lessons/model-under-pressure/#ai-local-results):**

- Vendor: 192/200 = 96%.
- Local total: (10 + 13 + 7 + 12)/50 = 84%; eight misses.
- Small deposits: (10 + 7)/25 = 68%; eight misses.
- Large deposits: 25/25 = 100%; no misses in this set.
- Scanner A: 23/25 = 92%; scanner B: 19/25 = 76%.

Have the learner explain the first row aloud: “Pathologists found metastases on 12 slides. The model found 10 and missed two.” Those 10 are true positives; the other two are false negatives. All 12 are known-positive slides. The scanner groups contain different slides, so the difference alone cannot prove that the scanner caused the misses.

**[Table 2](../../lessons/model-under-pressure/#ai-monthly-results):** July and August each have 17/25 small deposits detected and 25/25 large deposits detected. September has 12/25 and 25/25: 37/50 overall, or 74%. The stain change is a possible explanation for the decline. Compare the case mix, scanner use, slide quality and the pathologists' diagnoses before assigning a cause.

Generalizability concerns performance on new cases or in another setting. Drift concerns a later change. Brittleness concerns a large output change after a small input change. The lesson explains a paired-image brittleness test but does not perform one; monthly counts alone cannot prove brittleness.

**[Slider results in Table 3](../../lessons/model-under-pressure/#ai-calculated-outcomes):** at separation 2.0, sensitivity and specificity are about 88.1%. At 10% prevalence, expected counts round to 881 true positives, 119 false negatives, 1,073 false positives and 7,927 true negatives. PPV is about 45.1%. At 1% prevalence, counts round to 88, 12, 1,180 and 8,720, with PPV about 6.9%. The two detection rates stay fixed.

Ask the learner to point to the numerator and denominator before calculating a percentage. Sensitivity starts with the truly positive slides. Specificity starts with the truly negative slides. PPV starts with all the model's positive calls.

The calculator sets sensitivity and specificity to 1 / (1 + exp(-separation)). This is an assumption of the exercise, not an estimate from the 50-slide test. Expected true positives are 10,000 × prevalence × sensitivity. Expected false positives are 10,000 × (1 − prevalence) × (1 − specificity). PPV is true positives / (true positives + false positives). The display rounds counts to whole slides and calculates percentages before rounding.

**Records to review before patient use:** the task is to choose information that answers a question. Naming a broad topic such as “device status” would not identify what to review.

- **G1:** local slide-test results separated by site and deposit size. Compare the fraction of known-positive slides missed, rather than raw miss counts. Ask how different case mixes could affect the comparison.
- **G2:** the laboratory procedure assigning whole-slide review, error reporting and authority to pause use. Product instructions may require review, but local staff still need assigned responsibilities.
- **G3:** the version and settings saved with the particular result. Current settings or an overall performance table cannot reconstruct the earlier configuration.
- **G4:** the current regulatory authorization and instructions for use, compared with the proposed tissue types, scanners and role of the pathologist. Good local performance does not establish that the authorization covers the proposal.

Ask the learner to explain why a different record would leave the question unanswered. The feedback addresses each selected record separately. Changing a choice clears its previous feedback so the learner can check the revised answer.

## Common misconceptions

- **“Positive means the computer found it.”** Separate the pathologists' finding from the program's call. A known-positive slide can be missed.
- **“It found 84%, so 84% of its positive calls are right.”** Ask which group supplied the denominator. The positive-only test measures sensitivity, not PPV.
- **“The model keeps learning whenever it sees a slide.”** Ask whether the saved parameters are actually being changed. Running a trained model and training it are separate activities.
- **“The vendor found 96%, so we should too.”** Compare the scanners, stains and kinds of slides.
- **“September proves the stain caused the problem.”** Ask what other differences should be checked.
- **“A cluster is a diagnosis.”** Compare the diagnoses, gene activity and preparation methods within the cluster before deciding what it represents.
- **“The summary reads well, so it must be right.”** Find the supporting statement in each original report.

## Discussion prompts

1. Why test new slides after training? Look for an explanation that success on examples used to adjust the model does not show how it handles new cases.
2. Why did PPV fall when prevalence fell? Look for both parts: fewer truly positive slides and a larger negative group that still produces false alarms.
3. What would Dr. Rao need before patient use? Look for a defined job, representative local testing, a plan for human review, stopping rules and a review of device status.

## Group variant

Assign the laboratory director, reviewing pathologist, analyst and treating clinician roles. Each learner names one mistake they need to be able to detect and what information they would use. Let another learner explain the proposed checks to a patient in ordinary words.

## Assess transfer

Ask the learner how the first check would change for a new scanner versus a generated cancer summary. The answer should identify different possible mistakes and a different comparison for finding them.
