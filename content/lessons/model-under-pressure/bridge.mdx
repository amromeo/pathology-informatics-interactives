### Begin with the pathologists' answers

Before the local test, pathologists examine the slides independently of the detector and agree on which contain metastases. Their diagnoses are the **reference diagnoses** used to judge the program's answers. “Reference” tells us which comparison we are using; disagreements still need review.

All 50 slides in this first table contain metastases according to that review. We can therefore ask how many the detector finds and how many it misses.

- A **true positive** is a slide that really contains metastasis and that the detector correctly calls positive.
- A **false negative** is a slide that contains metastasis but that the detector misses.

A known-positive slide can become either of those outcomes when we test the detector. Calling all 50 slides “true positives” would assume that it found every one.

### Read one row before comparing the groups

The first row in **Table 1** concerns 12 slides with small deposits, scanned on scanner A. The detector found 10 and missed two. Its detection rate for that group is 10 divided by 12, or 83.3%.

This detection rate is called **sensitivity**: of all the slides that contain metastases, what fraction does the detector find? For the complete local set, the calculation is 42 divided by 50, or 84%. The other eight slides are misses. An 84% sensitivity does not mean that 84% of the detector's positive calls are correct; that is a different question, which we will examine with the sliders.

### Why test in this laboratory?

The vendor reported 96% detection on its positive slides. Local detection was lower from the first test. A model's ability to work on new cases or in a different laboratory is called **generalizability**. The lower local result is a reason to investigate how well this model carries over to your laboratory.

Images can differ because of scanners, stains, tissue preparation and the kinds of cases tested. The table alone cannot tell us which difference caused a miss. Read each row of Table 1, keeping small and large deposits separate. Ask where the detector missed the largest fraction of positive slides and which cases Dr. Rao should review first.

There are no cancer-free slides in this table. We cannot use it to count false alarms on negative slides or to calculate how often a positive call is correct. Those questions require negative slides too.
