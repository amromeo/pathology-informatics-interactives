# Faculty guide: The Missing CBC

## Learning objective

By the end of the lesson, the learner should be able to identify the hardware, software, network connections, and interfaces involved in electronic result reporting.

## Case mechanism

- The ED sees the CBC as pending and does not know whether testing is complete.
- The hematology lab checks the LIS and finds a final result verified at 09:42.
- The result reached the interface engine, where it joined a growing outbound queue.
- The interface server and application are running.
- A hospital firewall change blocks the connection from the interface server to the vendor-hosted EHR.
- Hospital IT corrects the firewall setting. The LIS team checks the queue and confirms that reporting resumes.
- The laboratory confirms that delayed and new reports reached the correct patient charts and reconciles results communicated during downtime.

## Discussion points

Ask learners what finding clears each earlier part of the path. A final result in the LIS clears neither the interface nor the EHR. A running interface application also does not prove that it can connect to the receiving system.

The lesson distinguishes the analyzer's analytical work from the postanalytic result-reporting pathway. The network is essential to delivering the report, but it is not part of the analyzer's analytical process.

Do not imply that every institution organizes support in the same way. The LIS team may be part of Hospital IT or may be housed in the pathology department. The roles shown here describe the work that must occur, not a mandatory reporting structure.

The technical terms are drawn from the API Pathology Informatics Introductory Course sessions on computer hardware, software, networking, and laboratory information systems. The case and artifacts are original.

## Suggested facilitation

1. Before opening the LIS record, ask what the ED actually knows.
2. During the map, ask learners to name the comparable systems at their institution.
3. In the evidence section, ask what each finding rules out before opening the next check.
4. After repair, ask why checking only the original CBC would be inadequate.
5. Use the hosting discussion to compare local support arrangements without claiming that one model is inherently safer or more reliable.

The debrief may be opened without completing every interaction. This lesson is formative practice, not a scored assessment.

## Preparing discussion with API slides

Use [Association for Pathology Informatics teaching slides: Computer Hardware, Software, Networking, and Data Files (PowerPoint)](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/assets/lms/jimharrison/PathInformatics%201%20Basics%20slides.pptx) by James H. Harrison, Jr., slides 5, 11, 14–15, to prepare the discussion after residents have explained their case decision. Ask: Separate the computer, network connection, operating system, and application. Which observation in the missing-result case would help you locate a failure among these layers? Have residents support their answer with a specific record or result from this lesson before comparing it with the slides.

These slides provide teaching background. Use the lesson's primary sources and current local policies for clinical criteria, legal requirements, and approval decisions; do not turn a slide example or numerical recommendation into a local requirement. If the PowerPoint link changes, locate the session on the [API teaching-slide page](https://www.pathologyinformatics.org/teaching-slide-sets).
