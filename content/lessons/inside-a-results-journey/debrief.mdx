## Debrief: What kept the CBC out of the chart?

The analyzer finished at 09:40. The LIS contained a CBC verified at 09:42, including a hemoglobin of 7.4 g/dL. Those records established that the laboratory had a result to communicate to the ED. Staff could provide that verified result through the downtime process while the analyst investigated electronic delivery.

The LIS analyst then followed the same report to the interface engine. Its queue contained 214 waiting results. Processor use was 18%, memory use was 46%, and the operating system and application were running. The delivery log showed a refused TCP connection to `10.20.30.40:5100`. The outgoing settings had been reloaded at 09:35, after maintenance.

The EHR analyst checked the receiving service. It was running at `10.20.30.40` and listening for laboratory messages on port `5000`. Its most recently accepted laboratory message was at 09:34. The sender's destination address matched the EHR's address. Its destination port, 5100, needed to be corrected to 5000.

That comparison identified the repair. The interface analyst corrected the saved destination port and tested the connection with the EHR analyst. Restarting the application with its old settings would send it back to port 5100. Repeating the CBC would add another report to the same waiting queue.

## What counted as restoration?

The original CBC needed to appear once in the correct chart. The team also needed to check the other queued reports, a newly released CBC and a report from another section using the same connection. Finally, staff compared the records of results communicated during downtime with the reports now available electronically.

Entering one CBC manually could make that report visible while the queue continued to grow. That is why the exercise gives this action only one passing check. Restoring the connection addresses both the original report and the other reports that depend on it.

The LIS and interface analysts follow the messages, correct the sending settings and check the queue. The EHR team checks the receiving service, accepted messages and chart display. The laboratory confirms that its complete reports reached the correct charts and accounts for downtime communications. Local department names may differ; the work still needs named people.

## Where do these systems run?

In this case, the LIS, interface engine and EHR run in the health system's own data center and communicate over its internal network. This is an **on-premises** arrangement. A computer in the same data center still needs the correct address and port to reach another computer's receiving service.

Other laboratories use outside computing services for part of this work. A **cloud** service supplies computing resources from shared infrastructure that can be allocated as needed. **Software as a service (SaaS)** means the vendor operates the application as a service. A **hybrid** arrangement combines institutional and outside systems. When drawing your own laboratory's reporting path, identify where each system runs and who supports its connections.

For your own laboratory, ask who checks each connection, who can repair it and how results are communicated during an interruption. PIER 1.4 uses these hardware, software and networking distinctions to help you investigate a laboratory problem.
