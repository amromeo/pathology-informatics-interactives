## Debrief: What kept the CBC out of the chart?

The analyzer finished at 09:40. The LIS held a CBC verified at 09:42, with a hemoglobin of 7.4 g/dL. So the laboratory had a result the ED needed. Staff could phone it through under the downtime procedure while the analyst worked out why it had not reached the chart.

The LIS analyst then followed the same report to the interface engine. Its queue held 214 waiting results. Processor use was 18%, memory use was 46%, and both the operating system and the application were running. The delivery log showed a refused TCP connection to `10.20.30.40:5100`. The outgoing settings had been reloaded at 09:35, after the maintenance.

The EHR analyst checked the receiving service. It was running at `10.20.30.40` and listening for laboratory messages on port `5000`. The last laboratory message it had accepted came in at 09:34. The sender had the right address but the wrong port: 5100 instead of 5000.

That comparison pointed to the fix. The interface analyst corrected the saved destination port and tested the connection with the EHR analyst. Restarting the application would only have reloaded the old setting and sent it back to port 5100. Repeating the CBC would have added one more report to the same stuck queue.

## When was the problem fixed?

The original CBC had to appear once, in the right patient's chart. The team also checked the other reports that had been waiting in the queue, a newly released CBC, and a report from another section that uses the same connection. Finally, staff compared their record of the results phoned during the outage with the reports that had now arrived electronically.

Typing this one CBC into the EHR would have made it visible, but the queue would have kept growing behind it. That is why manual entry passes only one of the checks. Fixing the connection gets the original report, and every report behind it, moving again.

The LIS and interface analysts follow the messages, correct the sending settings and check the queue. The EHR team checks the receiving service, the messages it accepted and how the report looks in the chart. The laboratory confirms that its complete reports reached the right charts and matches them against the calls made during the outage. Your hospital may divide this work differently, but each job needs a named person.

## Where do these systems run?

In this case, the LIS, interface engine and EHR all run in the health system's own data center and talk over its internal network. This is called an **on-premises** setup. Even inside one data center, a computer needs the right address and port to reach another computer's receiving service.

Other laboratories run some of these systems elsewhere. With a **cloud** service, the computers belong to an outside company and the hospital pays for as much computing power as it needs. With **software as a service (SaaS)**, the vendor runs the application itself and the hospital uses it over the network. A **hybrid** setup mixes the hospital's own systems with outside ones. When you draw your own laboratory's reporting path, note where each system runs and who looks after its connections.

For your own laboratory, find out who checks each connection, who can fix it, and how results reach clinicians while it is down.
